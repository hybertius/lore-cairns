# Retro Cairn: Games, Mods, Narrative & The Search For Interesting Things

## Terrain

What problem were we actually standing in?

Finding new RPGs when most of the obvious recommendations have already been played, dissected, modded, and often exhausted.

Not a shortage of games.

A shortage of surprising experiences.

What seemed confusing at the time?

Why so many highly praised modern titles felt less compelling despite being objectively large, polished, and expensive.

Why certain older or unusual games remained memorable years later.

What assumptions were present?

The common assumption that "more content" means "better RPG."

An additional assumption that narrative-heavy games necessarily provide better storytelling.

Both were quietly challenged.

Observation

What was noticed?

Many beloved games shared something beyond mechanics:

Kingdoms of Amalur

Alice: Madness Returns

Bastion

Transistor

Hades

Outer Wilds

Fenyx Rising

They possess a particular sense of identity.

They know what they are.

What changed understanding?

The realization that narrative importance is not the same as narrative volume.

A game can have hundreds of cutscenes and still feel exhausting.

A game can have almost none and remain unforgettable.

What joke accidentally carried an insight?

The comparison between Souls storytelling and an early Zergling rush.

Both force immediate participation.

Sometimes that creates engagement.

Sometimes it creates irritation.

The same mechanism can be genius or exhausting depending on what the traveller currently needs.

Importance

What seemed important then?

Finding a new RPG recommendation.

What was actually important?

Identifying the qualities that make a game remain memorable after completion.

What remained important afterwards?

World identity.

Atmosphere.

Narrative integration.

Respect for player pacing.

The feeling that a game is inviting the player somewhere rather than dragging them through a screenplay.

Future Traveller Notes

What might another traveller miss?

When Sloth says narrative is important, that does not automatically mean cutscenes are important.

Often the opposite.

Narrative works best when woven into interaction.

StarCraft II became an example of this.

Story exists.

It matters.

But it rarely interrupts the player's agency.

What mistake is easy to repeat?

Confusing cinematic density with storytelling quality.

What stone is worth placing?

Ask:

"How does the game tell its story?"

before asking:

"How much story does it have?"

Unexpected Findings

What became surprisingly useful?

Looking at games through narrative delivery rather than genre labels.

This immediately explained why:

Yakuza qualifies as an RPG in spirit.

Outer Wilds feels unlike other puzzle games.

Fenyx Rising succeeds where larger Ubisoft titles struggle.

Hades remains engaging despite constant repetition.

What observation kept returning?

Strong identity beats scale.

Repeatedly.

Did a What emerge?

Yes.

"What if the memorable part is not the content itself, but the relationship between gameplay and narrative?"

A certified small-to-medium What.

🫠

Persistence

What kept reappearing despite changing topics?

Craftsmanship.

Whether discussing mods, stories, communities, or games.

The discussion repeatedly returned to people who care deeply about what they're building.

What idea refused to go away?

That passion is visible.

Players notice.

Even when they cannot articulate why.

What concern survived multiple conversations?

Modern recommendation systems increasingly reward visibility instead of quality.

This is true for games.

This is true for mods.

This is true for many things.

Translation

What became easier to explain?

Why some games remain favorites despite obvious flaws.

Gothic.

Dragon's Dogma.

Kingdoms of Amalur.

Deus Ex.

They possess coherence.

The rough edges become part of their character.

What misunderstanding finally clicked?

That "good narrative" and "lots of narrative" are separate measurements entirely.

What metaphor proved unexpectedly useful?

The StarCraft II comparison.

Story functioning like support units rather than occupying the entire battlefield.

Friction

What resistance was encountered?

Modern modding culture.

Metrics.

Downloads.

Visibility.

Protectionism.

Burnout.

Was the resistance protecting something useful?

Sometimes.

Many creators are protecting years of unpaid work.

The concern is understandable.

Even when the resulting behaviour is unfortunate.

What assumption was challenged?

The assumption that highly recommended automatically means highly trustworthy.

Traffic can be purchased.

Visibility can be manufactured.

Curation still matters.

Stewardship

What needed maintaining rather than creating?

Personal archives.

Curated collections.

Mod repositories.

Instructions.

Backups.

The old traveller traditions.

What became more valuable through continuity?

Knowledge.

A personal understanding of which games, communities, and creators consistently produce worthwhile experiences.

What was handed off successfully?

Several overlooked recommendations and observations that may later help another traveller searching for unusual RPGs.

Shuckology

Which joke turned out not to be a joke?

"Maybe I've simply played too many games."

It was funny.

It was also largely true.

🫠

Which shuck concealed an observation?

The recurring StarCraft references.

Underneath them exists a consistent philosophy:

Players generally enjoy participation more than observation.

Was a loom detected?

Yes.

A moderate Narrative Loom.

It attempted to weave:

"More Story = Better Story."

Investigation revealed several loose threads.

Should Future Travellers be warned?

Absolutely.

If a recommendation contains the words:

cinematic

immersive storytelling

sixty hour narrative

award winning cutscenes

Future Traveller should immediately ask:

"Yes, but do I get to play?"
